# LOBELIA (Lobelia inflata) – INDIAN TOBACCO

## Master Monograph: Complete Integration Across All Departments

**Primary Species:** Lobelia inflata L. (Indian tobacco) — Annual/biennial native to eastern North America; slender herb with small pale flowers and characteristic **inflated seed pods**.[web:156][web:160]  
**Botanical Family:** Campanulaceae.[web:156]  
**Part Used:** **Aerial parts** (leaf, flowering tops, pods), fresh or dried.[web:154][web:155][web:160]  
**Harvesting & Processing:** Aerial parts collected when pods are well‑developed but still green; dried in thin layers with good airflow. Historically used as fresh plant extract for stronger activity.[web:155][web:160]  
**Quality Indicators:** Light‑green to brownish herb, characteristic pungent odor and acrid taste; properly dried material retains some color and sharpness, not musty.[web:155][web:160]  
**Storage:** Dried herb airtight, cool/dark; best within 1–2 years. Extracts stable several years in dark glass.[web:154][web:155]  
**Historical Context:** Core herb of the **Thomsonian and Eclectic** schools as a “depressing emetic,” bronchodilator, anti‑asthmatic, and relaxant for muscular tension; once used for smoking cessation and as a respiratory stimulant in poisonings. Modern pharmacology has revealed a **narrow therapeutic window and significant toxicity**, so mainstream references now warn against internal use.[web:154][web:155][web:162][web:158][web:168]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Key Constituents:**

- **Pyridine Alkaloids (0.2–0.4%):**  
  - **Lobeline** (α‑lobeline) — primary.[web:154][web:156][web:162]  
  - Isolobeline, lobelanidine, lobelanine, etc.  
- **Minor Constituents:** Flavonoids, resins, gum, and a small amount of volatile oil.[web:154][web:155]

**Mechanisms:**

- **Nicotinic Receptor Modulation:**  
  - Lobeline binds to **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** (nAChRs) with complex agonist/antagonist effects.[web:157][web:162][web:156]  
  - Low doses: mild stimulation of autonomic ganglia and carotid body, increasing respiratory rate and depth.[web:154][web:162]  
  - Higher doses: desensitization and depression of autonomic and CNS function.

- **Respiratory Effects:**  
  - Stimulates carotid body chemoreceptors → transient increase in respiration (historically used in respiratory failure/overdose).  
  - Bronchial smooth muscle relaxation and expectorant reflexes help clear mucus.[web:154][web:162][web:160]

- **Dopamine & Psychostimulant Modulation:**  
  - Lobeline redistributes presynaptic dopamine vesicles and reduces amphetamine‑induced dopamine release, modulating mesolimbic pathways.[web:157][web:162][web:156]  
  - Studied as potential aid in **nicotine dependence** and stimulant addiction; clinical results limited by side effects.[web:157][web:162]

## KEY MECHANISMS & USES (WITH RISK CONTEXT)

**1. Bronchodilator & Expectorant (Asthma / Bronchitis – HISTORICAL)**  
At low doses, lobelia relaxes bronchial smooth muscle, stimulates cough and expectoration, and can alleviate spasm.[web:154][web:155][web:162]  
Effect: Loosens mucus, eases breathing in spasmodic bronchitis or asthma when used with other antispasmodics and demulcents.[web:155]  
Timeline: Onset within minutes to hours; very dose‑sensitive.  
Modern significance: Given toxicity and availability of safer drugs/herbs (e.g., inhalers, theophylline, ephedra‑free formulas), **lobelia is no longer considered first‑line** and is often avoided.

**2. Emetic & “Rescue” Herb (OBSOLETE)**  
High doses induce nausea and vomiting; was used to “reverse poisoning” or force emesis.[web:155][web:160][web:164]  
Effect: Strong, unpleasant vomiting and autonomic symptoms.  
Modern significance: Intentional emesis with lobelia is **not acceptable practice**; safer, medically supervised protocols exist.

**3. Smoking Cessation / CNS Modulation**  
Lobeline, as a nAChR ligand and DA modulator, showed some early promise for smoking cessation, but clinical trials found **nausea and autonomic side‑effects too limiting** and efficacy insufficient.[web:157][web:162][web:164]

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION (HISTORICAL / PRACTITIONER‑ONLY)

Mainstream safety references: **“Likely unsafe” by mouth** at anything beyond very small amounts; no official safe dose established.[web:158][web:168][web:162][web:164][web:160]

**Traditional Low‑Dose Use (Herbalist Practice):**

- **Extract (1:5 in ~50–70% alcohol):**  
  - Low dose: **1–5 drops** up to 3–4× daily (i.e., micro‑dosing), often in formula.[web:155][web:160]  
  - Some Eclectic formulas historically used 5–15 drops per dose; this is now considered **high‑risk** and is avoided by most modern practitioners.

- **Crude Herb:**  
  - Historically 50–100 mg herb as low dose; emetic doses larger (not recommended).[web:164][web:168]

**Emetic Doses (OBSOLETE & UNSAFE):**

- Doses high enough to induce vomiting (multiple ml extract or grams herb) can cause severe toxicity; **not advised**.[web:155][web:160][web:168][web:164]

**Duration:**  

- **Short‑term only** (days to a week) when used at all. Prolonged use is not recommended.[web:160]

## ADVERSE EFFECTS & TOXICITY

**Common at moderate doses:**  

- Nausea, vomiting, salivation, sweating, dizziness, flushing, tremor, and throat/burning sensations.[web:154][web:158][web:168][web:164]  

**More severe at higher doses:**  

- Hypotension or hypertension, tachycardia or bradycardia, confusion, weakness, stupor, coma, possibly death.[web:154][web:158][web:168][web:164][web:162]  
- Respiratory depression and collapse, especially in vulnerable individuals.[web:162][web:164]

**Trial‑level data:**  

- In a human study, lobeline 0.5 mg caused nausea and burning; higher doses increased nausea, palpitations and autonomic side effects, limiting dose escalation.[web:154][web:157]

## CONTRAINDICATIONS & CAUTIONS (FULL DISCLOSURE)

**Absolute / Strong Contraindications (internal use):**

- **Pregnancy & Breastfeeding:**  
  - Lobelia is **likely unsafe**; risk of severe vomiting, cardiovascular and CNS toxicity; avoid completely.[web:158][web:168][web:160][web:162]

- **Children & Infants:**  
  - Increased sensitivity; avoid internal use entirely.[web:160][web:164]

- **Cardiovascular Disease:**  
  - Ischemic heart disease, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, uncontrolled hypertension or hypotension → lobelia can destabilize HR and BP.[web:158][web:164][web:162][web:160]

- **Seizure Disorders / Epilepsy:**  
  - Nicotinic agonists can lower seizure threshold; avoid.[web:160][web:164]

- **Severe Respiratory Disease / Shock:**  
  - Vulnerable to respiratory depression; some references explicitly list these as contraindicated.[web:162][web:164]

- **GI Disease:**  
  - Peptic ulcer, IBD, GI infection, severe GERD: lobelia’s emetic/irritant activity can aggravate.[web:158][web:164][web:168][web:160]

- **Liver or Kidney Disease:**  
  - Decreased clearance → higher toxicity risk.[web:164][web:160]

**Drug Interactions (Theoretical & Documented):**

- **Cardiac Medications:**  
  - Beta‑blockers, antiarrhythmics, digitalis: combined with lobelia’s cardiac effects can destabilize rhythm/BP.[web:160][web:164]

- **CNS Stimulants & Sympathomimetics:**  
  - Amphetamines, cocaine, pseudoephedrine: may precipitate convulsions or severe autonomic reactions when lobelia is used, especially at higher doses.[web:155][web:164][web:162]

- **CNS Depressants:**  
  - Sedatives, anesthetics, opioids: additive CNS and respiratory depression risk, especially if lobelia used near time of sedation.[web:155][web:162][web:164]

- **Nicotine & Smoking Cessation Drugs:**  
  - Nicotinic receptor cross‑talk; unpredictable synergy with varenicline, bupropion, heavy tobacco use.[web:162][web:160]
