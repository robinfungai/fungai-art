## BOTANICAL & IDENTIFICATION

**Primary Species:**  
Senna alexandrina Mill. (syn. Cassia senna L., C. angustifolia Vahl) — Shrub native to Northeast Africa and the Arabian Peninsula; cultivated in Sudan, Egypt, India.[web:9][web:19]

**Botanical Family:**  
Fabaceae (legume family).[web:9]

**Part Used:**  
Sennae folium (Senna leaf) — Dried leaflets; senna pods (fruits) are also used, generally slightly gentler than leaf.[web:9]

**Harvesting & Processing:**  
Mature leaves harvested during flowering/fruiting, dried rapidly in shade to preserve sennosides; stored in dry, dark conditions.[web:19]

**Quality Indicators:**  
Olive- to grey-green leaflets; characteristic odor, slightly bitter taste; standardized to defined sennoside A+B content (commonly 1.4–3% in EU monographs).[web:9][web:19]

**Storage:**  
Dry, airtight, away from light; sennoside content relatively stable for several years if properly stored.[web:19]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Active Compounds:**  

**Primary Bioactives (Anthraquinone Glycosides):**  
- **Sennosides A, B (and related):** Dimeric anthrone glycosides; main stimulant laxative actives.[web:9][web:19]

**Supporting Compounds:**  
- Flavonoids, mucilage, naphthalene glycosides, small amounts of other anthraquinones.[web:19]

**Mechanisms:**  
- **Stimulant Laxative:** In the colon, sennosides are metabolized by gut microbiota into rhein anthrone, which irritates colonic mucosa, stimulating peristalsis and secretion of fluid/electrolytes, increasing stool volume and speed of transit.[web:9][web:19]  
- **Onset:** Typically 6–12 hours after oral dosing; taken at bedtime for morning effect.[web:9]

---

## KEY MECHANISMS

**1. Stimulant Laxative (PRIMARY)**  
Effect: Produces bowel movement by enhancing peristalsis and reducing water reabsorption, softening stool and increasing frequency.[web:9][web:19]  
Timeline: 6–12 hours after dose; sometimes up to 24 hours.  
Clinical significance: Suitable for short-term treatment of acute or occasional constipation when bulk-forming or osmotic laxatives are insufficient or unsuitable.

**2. Impact on Electrolytes and Colon Tone (Long-Term Risk)**  
Chronic use can lead to electrolyte abnormalities (especially hypokalemia) and “cathartic colon” with reduced intrinsic motility.[web:9][web:19]  
Effect: Dependence on stimulant laxatives, worsening constipation on withdrawal.

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION

**Dosage Range (Adults):**  

**Senna Leaf (dried):**  
- Common effective dose: 15–30 mg sennosides per day, typically corresponding to about 0.5–2 g dried leaf depending on product standardization.[web:9]  
- Prepared as tea or standardized tablets.

**Tea/Infusion:**  
- 0.5–2 g senna leaf in 150 ml hot water, steeped 10–15 minutes; taken once daily, preferably at bedtime.[web:9][web:19]

**Granules/Tablets/Chewables:**  
- Dose adjusted to deliver 15–30 mg sennosides at bedtime.[web:9]

**Duration:**  
- Not advised for use beyond 1–2 weeks without medical supervision.[web:9][web:19]  
- For chronic constipation, address underlying causes rather than long-term senna use.

**Best Preparations & Protocols:**  

**Protocol 1: Short-Term Relief of Acute Constipation**  
- 15–30 mg sennosides via tea or standardized product at bedtime.  
- Evaluate effect; adjust dose but avoid exceeding recommended maximums.[web:9]  
- For use over 1–7 days while increasing hydration, fiber, and activity.

**Protocol 2: Pre-Procedure Purging (Under Medical Direction)**  
- Higher or repeated dosing may be used in combination regimens (e.g., bowel prep); always under clinical protocol.

## CONTRAINDICATIONS & INTERACTIONS

**Safety Profile:**  
GRADE C — Safe for short-term use in appropriate adults; significant risk with chronic use or in specific conditions.[web:9][web:19]

**Contraindications:**  
- Intestinal obstruction, ileus, acute surgical abdomen.  
- Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s, ulcerative colitis), appendicitis, severe dehydration.[web:9]  
- Unexplained abdominal pain of unknown origin.  
- Children under 12 years (for leaf preparations in typical EU monographs).[web:9][web:19]  
- Pregnancy and lactation: Some guidelines permit cautious short-term use; many practitioners prefer to avoid stimulant laxatives and use gentler options.[web:9][web:19]

**Cautions:**  
- Chronic use (>1–2 weeks) can lead to dependence and electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia), increasing risk of arrhythmias especially with cardiac glycosides or diuretics.[web:9][web:19]  
- Excess dosing can cause cramping, diarrhea, dehydration.

**Herb-to-Drug Interactions:**  
- **Cardiac Glycosides (digoxin):** Hypokalemia from senna may increase toxicity risk.[web:9][web:19]  
- **Diuretics, Corticosteroids, Licorice (high dose):** Additive hypokalemic potential.[web:19]  
- **Antiarrhythmics:** Electrolyte shifts can alter effect.

## CLINICAL NOTES & PRACTITIONER GUIDANCE

- **Clinical Hierarchy:** Use senna when constipation is causing significant distress and gentler methods have not yet worked, but always as a short-term bridge to sustainable bowel health (fiber, fluids, movement, microbiome).  
- **Formulation Tips:** Combine with carminatives (fennel, ginger, peppermint) to reduce griping; consider pairing with demulcents if dryness is an issue.  
- **Patient Education:** Clearly explain short-term nature; encourage bowel retraining with fiber, abdominal massage, hydration, and addressing medications that cause constipation (e.g., opioids).
