# BUTCHER’S BROOM (Ruscus aculeatus)

## Master Monograph: Complete Integration Across All Departments

**Primary Species:** Ruscus aculeatus L. (Butcher’s broom, Knee holly, Jew’s myrtle) — Small evergreen shrub native to the Mediterranean and Western Europe, with stiff, spiny cladodes (“false leaves”) and red berries.[web:139][web:141][web:146]  
**Botanical Family:** Asparagaceae (formerly Ruscaceae).![web:141]  
**Part Used:** **Rhizome and root (Rusci rhizoma)**; standardized extracts are typically saponin‑rich (ruscogenins).[web:141][web:146][web:139]  
**Harvesting & Processing:** Rhizomes dug, cleaned, sliced, and dried. Extracts produced with hydroalcoholic solvents and standardized to total ruscogenins (e.g., ~5–15%).[web:139][web:146]  
**Quality Indicators:** Firm, yellowish‑brown rhizome pieces; slight odor, bitter taste. Extracts should report % ruscogenins and daily dose equivalent.[web:139][web:146]  
**Storage:** Dried rhizome and extracts in airtight containers, cool/dark; stable several years when protected from moisture.[web:141][web:146]  
**Historical Context:** Traditional European remedy for **varicose veins, hemorrhoids, and “sluggish circulation”**; modern evidence supports use in **chronic venous insufficiency (CVI), hemorrhoids, and orthostatic hypotension**.[web:139][web:140][web:142][web:149]

## FUNCTIONAL FRAMEWORK

**Energetics (Western):**  
- Temperature: Neutral to slightly warming.  
- Taste: Bitter, slightly acrid.  
- Actions: Venotonic, vasoconstrictive (selective venous), capillary protective, mild diuretic, anti‑edema.[web:139][web:141][web:146]

**Primary Target Systems:**  
- **Venous System:** Chronic venous insufficiency, varicose veins, venous stasis, malleolar edema, venous cramps.[web:139][web:140][web:142][web:146]  
- **Hemorrhoidal Plexus:** Hemorrhoids and perianal venous congestion.[web:146][web:149]  
- **Lymphatics & Capillaries:** Edema of venous/lymphatic origin; capillary sealing, microcirculation support.[web:139][web:146]

**Core Principle:**  
“Butcher’s broom is a **venous alpha‑adrenergic agonist tonic**—tightening lax veins, reducing pooling and edema, and restoring tone in CVI and hemorrhoidal patterns, with good safety at standardized doses.”[web:139][web:140][web:143][web:146]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Key Constituents:**

- **Steroidal Saponins:** Ruscogenin, neoruscogenin, and related glycosides are primary actives.[web:139][web:146]  
- **Other Components:** Flavonoids, phytosterols, furanocoumarins (traces), etc.[web:139]

**Mechanisms:**

- **Venoconstrictive & Venotonic:**  
  Ruscus aculeatus acts as an **alpha‑adrenergic agonist**, directly activating postjunctional α1‑ and α2‑receptors in venous smooth muscle, and stimulating noradrenaline release at the vascular wall.[web:143]  
  Effect: Venous constriction, reduced venous capacity, less pooling in the legs, improved venous return.[web:139][web:143][web:146]

- **Capillary Protection & Anti‑edematous:**  
  Butcher’s broom improves lymphatic drainage and capillary sealing, reducing capillary permeability and edema.[web:139][web:146]  
  Clinical trials show decreased ankle and leg volume over time.[web:139][web:140]

- **Orthostatic Hypotension:**  
  Venoconstrictive properties suggest utility in **orthostatic hypotension (OH)** by reducing venous pooling upon standing; early clinical data are promising but limited.[web:143]

**Evidence Base:**

- Multiple double‑blind, placebo‑controlled trials and open trials demonstrate improvement in CVI symptoms (itching, ankle diameter, leg tension, cramping, edema).[web:139][web:140][web:146][web:149]  
- One randomized trial suggests butcher’s broom may be more effective than rutoside in CVI.[web:139]

## KEY MECHANISMS

**1. Chronic Venous Insufficiency & Varicose Veins (CVI)**  
By α‑adrenergic venoconstriction and capillary sealing, butcher’s broom reduces venous capacity and edema.[web:139][web:140][web:143][web:146][web:149]  
Effect: Decreased ankle circumference and leg volume; reduced heaviness, pain, cramps, itching, and tension in legs.[web:139][web:140]  
Timeline: Clinical trials ~12 weeks (3 months) show significant improvements vs placebo.[web:140]  
Clinical significance: Key herb for CVI, varicosities, venous leg symptoms.

**2. Hemorrhoids & Perianal Venous Congestion**  
Same venotonic and capillary protective mechanisms apply to hemorrhoidal plexus.[web:146][web:149]  
Effect: Reduced pain, swelling, bleeding, and prolapse symptoms; often in combination with flavonoids (e.g., hesperidin/diosmin).  
Timeline: Weeks to months for chronic hemorrhoids.

**3. Orthostatic Hypotension (OH) & Venous Pooling**  
Ruscus may ameliorate OH symptoms by reducing venous pooling; early review suggests “great promise” for OH, though more research needed.[web:143]  
Effect: Improved tolerance of standing, reduced dizziness and syncope episodes in susceptible individuals when combined with compression and lifestyle measures.  
Clinical significance: Potential niche use; should be used under medical supervision.

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION

**Standardized Extracts (Modern Use):**

- Typical CVI/hemorrhoid preparations:  
  - ~150 mg dry extract (3:1 or similar) standardized to **ruskogenins ~7–11 mg/day**, often combined with vitamin C and hesperidin.[web:139][web:140][web:146][web:149]  
  - Many products: 2–3 capsules/day totaling 100–300 mg extract.

- German monograph / RCT:  
  - Daily dose according to German monograph considered safe and effective over 3 months for CVI.[web:140]

**Traditional Root/Rhizome Decoction:**  
- Less commonly used now; historically 1–3 g/day dried rhizome in decoction, divided doses.

**Duration:**  
- CVI: 2–3 months minimum; longer use likely safe in many cases, but periodic reassessment recommended.[web:139][web:140][web:146]  
- Hemorrhoids: 4–12 weeks depending on severity.

## KEY BENEFITS & CONTRAINDICATIONS

**Benefits:**  
- **CVI / Varicose veins:** Improved symptoms and edema.[web:139][web:140][web:146][web:149]  
- **Hemorrhoids:** Symptom relief.![web:146][web:149]  
- **Potential OH adjunct:** Venous tone support.[web:143]

**Safety Overview:**  
- Ruscus extracts are generally well tolerated at recommended doses; adverse events usually mild (GI upset, nausea, headache).[web:139][web:141][web:152][web:146]

**⚠️ CRITICAL CAUTIONS**

- **Signs Requiring Immediate Medical Care:**  
  EMA notes that if there is **inflammation of the skin, subcutaneous induration, ulcers, sudden swelling of one or both legs, cardiac or renal insufficiency**, the patient should seek immediate medical attention rather than self‑treat with Ruscus.[web:141]  
  (Red flag for possible DVT, heart failure, etc.)

- **Pregnancy & Lactation:**  
  - Limited data; some monographs advise avoiding Ruscus in pregnancy due to vasoconstrictive/adrenergic actions; use only if benefit outweighs risk under medical supervision.[web:141][web:152]

**MAJOR CAUTIONS**

- **Hypertension & Cardiovascular Disease:**  
  - Theoretical concern: α‑adrenergic vasoconstriction may impact BP; most studies show good tolerance in normotensive patients, but caution with uncontrolled hypertension or major CV disease.

- **Renal & Cardiac Insufficiency:**  
  - EMA warns that patients with cardiac or renal insufficiency presenting leg edema should **not** self‑treat with Ruscus; physician evaluation mandatory.[web:141]

**Drug Interactions:**  
- Theoretical interaction with other **vasoactive drugs** (e.g., alpha agonists/antagonists, antihypertensives); monitor BP and symptoms.  
- No robust CYP data suggesting strong metabolic interactions.
