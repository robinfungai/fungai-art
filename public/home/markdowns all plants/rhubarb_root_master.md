# RHUBARB ROOT (Rheum palmatum / Rheum officinale) – DA HUANG

## Master Monograph: Complete Integration Across All Departments

**Primary Species:** Rheum palmatum L., Rheum officinale Baill., and related species (Chinese rhubarb; Da Huang) — Large perennial herb native to China and Tibet; thick rhizomes and roots are used medicinally.[web:105][web:108][web:111][web:118]  
**Botanical Family:** Polygonaceae.![web:111]  
**Part Used:** **Rhizome and root** (dried); leaf stalks of culinary rhubarb are different and not used medicinally (leaves are toxic).[web:111][web:118]  
**Harvesting & Processing:** Roots dug from 4–8‑year‑old plants; washed, sliced, and dried in sun or low heat. TCM differentiates raw vs wine‑fried vs cooked forms for different actions (strong purgative vs more blood‑moving/heat‑clearing).![web:105][web:108][web:111]  
**Quality Indicators:** Dense, firm, yellowish to reddish‑brown root slices with distinct radiating pattern and strong, characteristic odor; high anthraquinone content.[web:105][web:111]  
**Storage:** Airtight containers, cool and dry; stable for several years if properly dried.[web:111]  
**Historical Context:** Classic TCM herb for over 2000 years as one of the primary **purgatives and heat/toxin drainers**, used in acute excess/heat conditions, constipation, dysentery, jaundice, and some febrile diseases.[web:105][web:108][web:111]

## TCM FRAMEWORK

**Pin Yin:** Da Huang (大黄).  
**TCM Category:** Purgative herb (Downward‑Draining, especially Heat‑Clearing).![web:105][web:108]

**TCM Meridians:** Heart, Liver, Stomach, Large Intestine (some sources also Kidney).[web:105][web:108][web:111]

**Taste:** Bitter, Sour.

**Actions (Traditional):**  
- Drains heat and **purges accumulations** (strong laxative).

- Cools blood, stops bleeding (when charred/prepared).  
- Moves blood and dispels blood stasis.  
- Clears heat‑toxin (for abscesses, sores, and hot infections).[web:105][web:108][web:111]

“Da Huang is a **cold, bitter purgative** that drives out internal heat and accumulation, cleanses the bowels, drains damp‑heat from Liver/Gallbladder, moves blood, and clears toxins—but must be used **short‑term and with great respect** due to its drug‑strength purgative nature and potential toxicity with long‑term use.”[web:105][web:108][web:111][web:118]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Key Constituents:**

- **Anthraquinone Glycosides:** Emodin, rhein, chrysophanol, aloe‑emodin, sennidin/rhein anthrones — major laxative actives.[web:111][web:118]  
- **Tannins:** Contribute astringent, anti‑diarrheal actions at lower doses.  
- **Other Constituents:** Stilbenes, flavonoids, organic acids, and minor constituents with anti‑inflammatory, anti‑microbial, and nephroprotective actions.[web:111][web:118]

**Mechanisms:**

- **Stimulant Laxative:**  
  - Anthraquinone glycosides are hydrolyzed by gut flora to active aglycones, which directly stimulate colonic motility, increase secretion, and reduce water reabsorption.  
  - Onset: ~6–8 hours after ingestion (typical of stimulant laxatives).[web:111][web:118][web:121]

- **Anti‑inflammatory & Anti‑bacterial:**  
  - Emodin and others exhibit antibacterial activity (e.g., against dysentery bacilli) and reduce inflammatory mediators, explaining use in dysentery and damp‑heat bowel patterns.[web:111][web:118]

- **Hepatobiliary & Renal:**  
  - Drains inflammation from **liver, large intestine, and kidney** in TCM framing; experimental studies support liver‑protective and nephroprotective effects at appropriate doses, but also potential toxicity with chronic overuse.[web:111][web:118]

- **Potential Cardio / Other:**  
  - Anthraquinones may affect cardiac conduction and electrolytes via potassium loss with long‑term use; this is a concern with all stimulant laxatives.[web:111][web:118][web:121]

## KEY MECHANISMS

**1. Strong Purgative for Heat‑Constipation & Accumulation**  
Da Huang is among the strongest herbal purgatives; used for **constipation with heat, abdominal fullness, high fever, and acute toxicity** scenarios.[web:105][web:108][web:111][web:118]  
Effect: Strong bowel evacuation within ~6–8 hours, reduction of abdominal distension and heat‑related symptoms.  
Timeline: Single‑dose or short‑course; not for chronic daily use.  
Clinical significance: Herb of choice in **acute, excess, heat/constipation**—similar to drug‑level stimulant laxatives; inappropriate for deficient, cold, or long‑term constipation.

**2. Damp‑Heat Drainer for Liver, Gallbladder, and Intestines**  
Used in formulas for **jaundice, gallstones, acute dysentery, and high fevers** due to damp‑heat accumulation.[web:105][web:108][web:111][web:118]  
Effect: Clears damp‑heat, reduces bilirubin and stagnation, and assists in clearing infection via purgation and antimicrobial action.  
Timeline: Short‑term, formula‑based use.

**3. Blood‑Moving & Toxin‑Clearing (Prepared Forms)**  
Wine‑fried or cooked Da Huang has a stronger blood‑moving and heat‑clearing effect with somewhat reduced purgation; used for blood stasis, trauma, and some liver/toxin patterns.[web:105][web:108][web:111]  
Effect: Moves stagnant blood, reduces pain and masses in formulas, clears heat‑toxin in deep tissues.  
Clinical significance: Used under TCM supervision for complex internal patterns.

**4. Anti‑bacterial / Dysentery & Acute Infections**  
Due to antibacterial and purgative actions, Da Huang can be part of formulas for **acute bacterial dysentery and acute infectious diarrhea with heat**.[web:111][web:118]  
Effect: Clears pathogens and toxins via purgation; reduces inflammation in gut.

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION

**Important:** Rhubarb root is **NOT** a gentle bowel herb; think of it as a drug‑strength purgative.

**TCM Dosage (Dried Root):**

- Internal use typically **3–12 g/day** in decoction, depending on indication and formula; lower doses for blood‑moving/heat‑clearing use, higher for strong purgation.[web:105][web:108][web:111]  
- Decoction often added in last 5–10 minutes of boiling (to preserve anthraquinones).

**Western Herbal Dosing:**

- Dried root in capsule: often 250–500 mg, 1–2× daily short‑term (similar to other stimulant laxatives).  
- Extract: 1:5 root — **0.5–2 ml at bedtime** as needed, short term; combined with other herbs to soften effect (e.g., carminatives, demulcents).

**Duration:**  

- Short‑term or occasional only.  
- “Rating: Yellow, due to problems with long‑term usage.”[web:111][web:118]  
- Long‑term use can cause **hypertrophy of liver, thyroid and stomach, dependence, electrolyte disturbances, and GI damage**.[web:111][web:118][web:121]

**Example Protocols (TCM‑style; all under supervision)**

**Protocol 1: Acute Heat‑Constipation with Abdominal Distension**  
- Da Huang 3–12 g in decoction with Mang Xiao and other heat‑clearing herbs, once daily until bowels move, then stop or sharply reduce.  
- Monitor for cramping, dehydration.

**Protocol 2: Acute Damp‑Heat Dysentery**  
- Lower dose Da Huang in formula with Huang Lian, Huang Qin, etc., for short‑term use until infection clears; not used as a solo herb.

**Protocol 3: Blood Stasis & Heat‑Toxin (Prepared Rhubarb)**  
- Wine‑fried Da Huang in moderate dose within formulas for cirrhosis, trauma, or deep tissue heat‑stasis, under TCM guidance.[web:111]

**Protocol 4: Western Short‑Term Constipation Relief**  
- 250–500 mg root (or equivalent extract) at bedtime as needed, no more than a few days in a row; avoid chronic use.

## KEY BENEFITS & CONTRAINDICATIONS

**Benefits:**  
- **Powerful stimulant laxative** for acute constipation due to heat/accumulation.[web:105][web:108][web:111][web:118]  
- Damp‑heat and toxin drainage in acute infection and liver/gallbladder disorders (in formula).[web:105][web:108][web:111]  
- Some nephroprotective/anti‑inflammatory actions in experimental kidney failure when used appropriately.[web:111]

**Safety & Toxicology:**

- Rated “Yellow” due to problems with **long‑term usage**, including organ hypertrophy and GI side effects.[web:111][web:118]  
- Anthraquinone‑type stimulant laxatives can cause dependence and are not for chronic daily use.[web:111][web:118][web:121]

**⚠️ ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATIONS**

- **Pregnancy & Nursing:**  
  - Do **not** use in pregnancy or breastfeeding; strong purgative and blood‑moving actions; potential harm to fetus/infant.[web:111][web:118][web:121]

- **Bowel Obstruction / Severe Abdominal Pain of Unknown Origin:**  
  - Contraindicated due to risk of perforation/aggravation.[web:111][web:121]

- **Severe Deficiency / Frail Patients:**  
  - Not for weak, cold, or severely depleted individuals; can worsen deficiency.[web:111][web:118]

- **Gout / Severe Kidney Disease:**  
  - Avoid in gout and serious renal impairment.[web:111][web:118]

**MAJOR CAUTIONS**

- **Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD):**  
  - Use **only under supervision**; stimulant laxatives can exacerbate inflammation in some contexts.[web:111][web:118]

- **Long‑Term Use:**  
  - Can cause liver, thyroid, and stomach hypertrophy, nausea, griping, abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea; anthraquinone dependence, electrolyte loss, and potential colon damage.[web:111][web:118][web:121]

- **Electrolyte Imbalance & Cardiac Risk:**  
  - Stimulant laxatives lower potassium; low K+ can increase risk of digoxin toxicity and cardiac arrhythmias.[web:121]

**Drug Interactions:**  

- **Digoxin (Lanoxin):**  
  - Stimulant laxatives decrease potassium; low K+ increases digoxin toxicity risk.[web:121]  

- **Other Laxatives:**  
  - Additive effect; higher risk of dehydration and electrolyte imbalance.  

- **Diuretics & Corticosteroids:**  
  - Additive potassium loss.

- **Anticoagulants & Surgery:**  
  - Potential increased bleeding with associated bowel irritation; stop before surgery as for other stimulant laxatives.
