Primary Species Rubus idaeus L.  
Raspberry Leaf, Red Raspberry Leaf  
Native to Europe and temperate Asia, now widely naturalized and cultivated. A thorny bramble whose leaves are harvested before or during flowering, then dried quickly to preserve tannins and aromatic green vitality.

Botanical Family  
Rosaceae rose family

Part Used  
Leaf primarily.

Harvesting Processing  
Young to mid-mature leaves are gathered in clean conditions, ideally before full fruiting, then dried promptly with good airflow. Over-browned material loses vibrancy and astringent quality.

Quality Indicators  
Green to grey-green leaf, mildly sweet-herbaceous aroma, clean astringent taste, low stem content, no mildew or excessive browning.

Storage  
Airtight glass or paper-lined dry storage away from heat and light; generally maintains quality 12–18 months.

Historical Context  
Long used in Western herbalism as a mineral-rich astringent uterine tonic and digestive ally. Commonly associated with pregnancy support, cycle regulation, loose stools, and postpartum recovery.

KEY MECHANISMS

1. Uterine Tonic Support  
Raspberry leaf is one of the classic uterine-support herbs in Western herbalism. It is traditionally used to support tone before birth, after birth, and through menstrual life rather than forcing hormonal change.

2. Menstrual / Pelvic Tissue Support  
Often used where there is pelvic laxity, mild cramping, or a need for long-term reproductive nourishment and tone.

3. Gentle Astringent Digestive Use  
The leaf can be useful for mild diarrhea or irritated bowel states where a soothing yet toning tea is appropriate.

4. Postpartum Recovery  
Traditionally included in formulas intended to help tissues recover after childbirth.

CONTRAINDICATIONS INTERACTIONS

Safety Profile  
Generally considered safe and food-like in typical tea doses.

Major Cautions  
- Pregnancy timing: Commonly used in pregnancy, but some practitioners prefer avoiding strong use in early pregnancy and reserving tonic use for later stages.  
- Constipation / marked dryness: Its astringent nature may be less ideal where tissues are already overly dry or bound.  
- Medication interactions: Significant interactions are not commonly emphasized, though routine medication review is still good practice.

Herb-to-Herb Pairings  
- Synergistic: Nettle, oatstraw, rose, lady’s mantle, shatavari, chamomile.  
- Caution: Pair thoughtfully with strong astringents if constipation or dryness is already present.
