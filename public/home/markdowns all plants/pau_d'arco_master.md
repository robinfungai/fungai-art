# PAU D’ARCO (Handroanthus / Tabebuia impetiginosus)

## Master Monograph: Complete Integration Across All Departments

**Primary Species:** Handroanthus impetiginosus (Mart. ex DC.) Mattos (formerly Tabebuia impetiginosa / T. avellanedae; common names: Pau d’arco, Lapacho, Taheebo, Ipê roxo, Pink Trumpet Tree) — Large deciduous tree native to Central and South America, especially the Amazon and surrounding regions.[web:59][web:61][web:67] Inner **bark** is the main medicinal part.  
**Botanical Family:** Bignoniaceae.[web:61]  
**Part Used:** Inner bark (phloem) and adjacent wood (“taheebo/lapacho”).[web:61][web:64][web:67]  
**Harvesting & Processing:** Inner bark stripped in narrow bands to avoid girdling; dried in shade with good airflow; cut or milled into chips or powder. Sustainability is a major concern—prefer certified sustainable or plantation sources.[web:61][web:64][web:67]  
**Quality Indicators:** Clean bark chips, reddish‑brown to purple‑brown; mildly aromatic, slightly bitter/astringent taste; free from mold and excessive outer bark. Standardized extracts should indicate species and lapachol/beta‑lapachone control (often low‑lapachol modern products).[web:59][web:64]  
**Storage:** Airtight container, cool and dark; retains potency 1–2 years when dry and protected from heat and humidity.[web:64][web:67]  
**Historical Context:** Traditional use among Indigenous peoples of South America and later in folk medicine throughout Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, etc., for **fevers, infections, wounds, dysentery, malaria, ulcers, cancer, and chronic inflammatory diseases**.[web:59][web:61][web:64][web:67] In modern herbalism, known for anticandidal, antifungal, antimicrobial, and potential anticancer properties—but with important safety caveats.[web:64][web:66][web:70]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Key Phytochemicals:**

- **Naphthoquinones:**  
  - **Lapachol**  
  - **β‑Lapachone**  
  - Related naphthoquinones (e.g., dehydro‑α‑lapachone, etc.)[web:59][web:64][web:70]  
  Actions: **Antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anti‑parasitic, anti‑inflammatory, antitumor.** Also responsible for much of the **toxicity** (GI irritation, bleeding risk, fetotoxicity, reproductive toxicity).[web:59][web:64][web:62][web:66]

- **Other Constituents:**  
  - Flavonoids, phenolic acids, iridoids, quinoid and benzenoid compounds.[web:59][web:69]  
  - Tannins and other polyphenols contribute astringent, anti‑inflammatory, and antioxidant activity.[web:59][web:64]

**Mechanisms (Modern Research):**

- **Antimicrobial / Antifungal / Antiparasitic:**  
  Pau d’arco extracts and lapachol/β‑lapachone show broad in vitro activity against **Candida spp., dermatophytes, bacteria (gram+ and gram–), viruses, and parasites**.[web:59][web:64][web:70] Mechanisms include disruption of microbial energy metabolism, redox cycling, DNA damage, and membrane disruption.[web:59][web:64]

- **Antitumor / Cytotoxic:**  
  Naphthoquinones exhibit cytotoxicity against various tumor cell lines; mechanisms include topoisomerase inhibition, ROS generation, apoptosis induction, and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase interactions (notably for β‑lapachone).[web:59][web:64][web:66] However, **human clinical evidence for cancer benefit is very limited**, and toxicity (bleeding, GI, reproductive) is a major concern.[web:66][web:68]

- **Anti‑inflammatory / Analgesic:**  
  Pau d’arco and its constituents are anti‑inflammatory in vitro and in vivo, reducing edema, prostaglandins (e.g., PGE2), and pain responses in animal models; mechanisms may resemble NSAID‑like modulation of prostaglandin pathways.[web:59][web:60][web:64][web:69]

- **Antiplatelet / Anticoagulant:**  
  Lapachol reduces platelet aggregation and can interfere with vitamin K‑dependent coagulation pathways, **increasing bleeding risk**, especially at doses >1.5 g/day in some reports.[web:62][web:65][web:57][web:68][web:66]

- **Metabolism & Enzymatic Interactions:**  
  β‑Lapachone is metabolized via COMT and CYP450s; may affect drug metabolism and redox pathways.[web:65][web:59]

## KEY MECHANISMS

**1. Broad Antimicrobial & Antifungal Action (Naphthoquinones)**  
Lapachol and β‑lapachone exhibit strong in vitro activity against Candida, dermatophytes, various bacteria, viruses, and parasites.[web:59][web:64][web:70]  
Effect: Potentially useful against **chronic fungal infections (e.g., candidiasis), recurrent bacterial infections, some viral states, and parasitic burdens**, especially in gut and skin contexts.[web:59][web:64][web:63][web:70]  
Timeline: Mechanistic data suggest relatively rapid microbial inhibition; clinical anecdote suggests weeks for chronic conditions.  
Clinical significance: **Promising but double‑edged**—due to toxicity risk, most clinicians prefer safer antifungal/antimicrobial herbs for long‑term internal use and reserve pau d’arco as short‑term or topical, or avoid it entirely when safer options exist.

**2. Anti‑inflammatory & Analgesic (NSAID‑like PGE2 Modulation)**  
Pau d’arco extracts reduce edema and pain in models of inflammation, with mechanisms overlapping NSAID‑like prostaglandin suppression and other anti‑inflammatory pathways.[web:59][web:60][web:64][web:69]  
Effect: Analgesic effects, including in primary dysmenorrhea (PDM): an open‑label trial with **1050 mg/day Tabebuia avellanedae for 8 weeks** in women with PDM showed **significant reductions in pain intensity** and was generally safe and moderately tolerated, with normal lab markers (PT, CBC, etc.) and mostly mild adverse events.[web:60]  
Timeline: Pain relief within hours in the PDM trial (NSAID‑like time course), with sustained reductions over 8 weeks.[web:60]  
Clinical significance: Potential low‑dose, monitored alternative/adjunct for dysmenorrhea and other pain states—but long‑term safety and broader populations remain insufficiently studied.

**3. Antitumor / Cytotoxic (Experimental, High‑Risk)**  
Naphthoquinones exert potent cytotoxic and antitumor effects in vitro and in animal models, but **human cancer data are sparse and mixed**, and toxicity has limited development.[web:59][web:66][web:64]  
Effect: Potential to inhibit tumor growth via ROS, apoptosis, topoisomerase inhibition, and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase pathways; but also risk of anemia, coagulopathy, reproductive toxicity, and other harms.[web:66][web:59][web:64]  
Timeline: Experimental, not clinically defined; **not recommended as stand‑alone cancer therapy**.  
Clinical significance: Should **not** be positioned as a primary anticancer treatment; any use must be oncology‑supervised and risk‑benefit weighed carefully. Many integrative cancer centers caution against unsupervised use.[web:66]

**4. Antiplatelet / Anticoagulant (Bleeding Risk)**  
Lapachol reduces platelet aggregation and interferes with coagulation; high doses have been associated with increased bleeding risk in human and animal data.[web:62][web:65][web:57][web:68][web:66]  
Effect: Prolonged bleeding time, increased bruising, potential hemorrhagic risk, especially with concurrent anticoagulant/antiplatelet drugs or herbs.[web:57][web:62][web:65][web:68]  
Timeline: Risk increases with higher doses and chronic use; >1.5 g/day lapachol‑rich preparations particularly problematic.[web:62][web:68][web:64]  
Clinical significance: **Major safety driver**—critical to screen for anticoagulants, surgery, bleeding disorders, and pregnancy.

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION

**Important:** There is **no universally accepted safe medicinal dose**, and many authorities consider pau d’arco **possibly unsafe** at higher doses, especially for long‑term use.[web:57][web:58][web:68][web:66] Modern products often **limit lapachol content** to reduce toxicity.[web:59][web:64]

**Traditional Decoction:**

- **Bark Decoction (Folk Ranges):**  
  1–2 teaspoons (≈2–4 g) dried inner bark per 250 ml water; simmer 15–30 minutes; 1–3 cups daily in short courses of days to a few weeks.[web:63][web:64][web:70]  
  Some older sources used much higher doses (e.g., 10–15 g/day), but these are now considered **unsafe** due to lapachol toxicity.[web:64][web:68]

**Modern Encapsulated Bark / Extract (Example Data):**

- **1050 mg/day encapsulated Tabebuia avellanedae** for 8 weeks in women with primary dysmenorrhea was generally safe and moderately tolerated, with significant pain reduction but some mild adverse events.[web:60]  
- Many commercial products provide **500–1500 mg/day** bark or extract; composition (lapachol content) is highly variable.[web:58][web:64][web:68]

**Topical Use:**

- Strong decoctions applied externally for fungal skin infections, wounds, ulcers, and impacted areas; typically better safety margin than high‑dose internal use.[web:64][web:63][web:70]

**Duration:**

- **Short‑term:** days to a few weeks for acute infections or short trials in dysmenorrhea, with monitoring.[web:60][web:63][web:57]  
- **Long‑term use** is problematic due to cumulative toxicity risk and should be avoided or strictly supervised.

**Example Protocols (Conservative):**

**Protocol 1: Short‑Course Antifungal / Antimicrobial Adjunct (CAUTIOUS)**  
- 2–4 g/day bark decoction, divided into 2–3 doses, for **7–14 days**, combined with safer antifungals/antimicrobials (e.g., garlic, thyme, berberine herbs) and gut‑supportive measures.  
- Reserve for cases where safer alternatives are insufficient and with full risk disclosure.

**Protocol 2: Primary Dysmenorrhea Trial (BASED ON STUDY)**  
- 1050 mg/day encapsulated Tabebuia avellanedae for up to 8 weeks as in the open‑label trial, with monitoring of symptoms and side effects.[web:60]  
- Avoid in patients at bleeding risk or on anticoagulants.

**Protocol 3: Topical Antifungal / Wound Wash**  
- Strong decoction (e.g., 10–15 g bark in 500–750 ml water) simmered 20–30 minutes; cooled and used externally on fungal infections, ulcers, or as a wash—once or twice daily.[web:63][web:64]  
- Minimizes systemic lapachol exposure relative to high‑dose internal use.

**Protocol 4: Cancer / Chronic Inflammatory Disease (NOT STAND‑ALONE)**  
- If used at all, only within oncology or specialist supervision; low‑dose, standardized, lapachol‑controlled extracts with careful monitoring of CBC, liver function, coagulation, and adverse events.[web:59][web:66][web:64]  
- Many integrative practitioners choose alternative, safer immunomodulatory herbs/mushrooms instead.

## KEY BENEFITS & CONTRAINDICATIONS

**Key Potential Benefits (with caveats):**

- Broad **antifungal / anticandidal, antibacterial, antiviral, antiparasitic** actions (strong in vitro).[web:59][web:64][web:63][web:70]  
- **Anti‑inflammatory and analgesic** effects; early clinical data for dysmenorrhea pain reduction.[web:59][web:60][web:64][web:69]  
- **Antitumor** activity in experimental models (not yet reliably demonstrated in humans; safety concerns significant).[web:59][web:66][web:64]

**⚠️ CRITICAL CONTRAINDICATIONS / HIGH‑RISK POPULATIONS**

- **Pregnancy – CONTRAINDICATED:**  
  Lapachol shows **teratogenic and abortifacient effects in animal studies**; pau d’arco is considered **contraindicated in pregnancy** by multiple sources.[web:62][web:66][web:68][web:57]  
  Topical use also not recommended due to lack of safety data.[web:62]

- **Breastfeeding – CONTRAINDICATED / AVOID:**  
  Insufficient reliable data; major references advise **avoiding use** during breastfeeding.[web:57][web:62][web:68]

- **Bleeding Disorders / Surgery / Anticoagulant or Antiplatelet Therapy:**  
  Pau d’arco **slows blood clotting**, with an increased risk of bruising and bleeding; lapachol reduces platelet aggregation and may interfere with vitamin K.[web:57][web:62][web:65][web:68][web:66]  
  Must be discontinued **at least 2 weeks before surgery**.[web:57]  
  Contraindicated or strongly cautioned with: warfarin, DOACs, heparins, aspirin, clopidogrel, and anticoagulant herbs (ginkgo, garlic, turmeric, etc.).[web:57][web:62][web:65]

- **History of Significant Liver Disease, Severe Anemia, or Reproductive Issues:**  
  High, chronic lapachol dosing caused **anemia and reproductive toxicity** in animals (seminal vesicle weight loss, fetotoxicity).[web:66][web:64][web:59]  
  Avoid in these populations.

**MAJOR CAUTIONS**

- **High Doses / Long‑term Use – POSSIBLY UNSAFE:**  
  High doses can cause **severe nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness, and severe bleeding**.[web:57][web:68][web:64][web:66]  
  Chronic high lapachol exposure increases risk of anemia and other toxicities.[web:66][web:64]

- **Drug Interactions:**  
  - Anticoagulant/antiplatelet drugs and herbs → increased bleeding risk.[web:57][web:62][web:65][web:68]  
  - Potential interactions via CYP systems and COMT (β‑lapachone metabolism), though specific isoforms and clinical impact remain unclear.[web:65][web:59]

- **Quality Variability:**  
  Products vary widely in lapachol content; some have very low lapachol (safer but potentially less potent), others may be high and dangerous.[web:64][web:68][web:58]

**Herb‑to‑Herb Interactions**

- **Avoid Combining With:**  
  - Strong anticoagulant/antiplatelet herbs: **Ginkgo, garlic, turmeric, ginger**, etc., due to additive bleeding risk.[web:62][web:65][web:57]  
  - Multiple strong hepatotoxic herbs in high doses.  

- **Potential Synergy (Short‑term, carefully dosed):**  
  - With **gut antifungals/antimicrobials** (berberine herbs, oregano, thyme) but only in short courses and at conservative doses, with bleeding risk monitored.

- Pau d’arco trees are heavily logged for timber and bark; overharvesting and illegal logging are documented concerns.[web:67]
