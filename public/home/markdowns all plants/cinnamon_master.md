# CINNAMON (Cinnamomum verum / C. cassia)

## Master Monograph: Complete Integration Across All Departments

**Primary Species:**  
- **Cinnamomum verum** (Ceylon/“true” cinnamon).  
- **Cinnamomum cassia** (Chinese cassia), plus C. burmannii, C. loureiroi etc. as “cassia cinnamons” with higher coumarin.[web:176][web:180][web:184]  

**Botanical Family:** Lauraceae.  
**Part Used:** **Inner bark** (quills, chips, powder) and **essential oil** from bark; leaf oil sometimes used separately.[web:180]  
**Harvesting & Processing:** Bark peeled from young shoots, outer cork removed, inner bark dried into quills; milled to powder or extracted. Cassia typically thicker, more pungent.[web:176][web:184]  
**Quality Indicators:** Warm, sweet, aromatic bark with characteristic cinnamon odor; true cinnamon has thin, multi‑layered quills, milder flavor; cassia thicker quills, stronger, hotter taste.[web:184][web:180]  
**Storage:** Whole quills/powder in airtight containers, cool/dark; essential oil in dark glass away from heat/light.[web:180]  
**Historical Context:** Ancient spice used in Ayurveda, TCM, and Western herbalism for **digestive warming, circulatory stimulation, menstrual support, and blood sugar modulation**, as well as sacred and culinary roles.[web:176]

## FUNCTIONAL FRAMEWORK

**Energetics:**  
- Temperature: Warming to hot.  
- Taste: Sweet, pungent, aromatic, slightly astringent.  
- Actions: Digestive carminative, warming circulatory stimulant, mild antimicrobial, **insulin‑sensitizing** (cassia), astringent, uterine stimulant (at higher doses).[web:176][web:180]

**Primary Target Systems:**  
- **Digestive:** Cold, sluggish digestion, gas, bloating, diarrhea from cold damp.[web:180]  
- **Metabolic:** Impaired glucose tolerance, features of metabolic syndrome (cassia).[web:176][web:180]  
- **Circulatory:** Cold extremities, mild hypotension/weak circulation.  
- **Gynecological:** Menstrual cramps/amenorrhea with cold pattern (traditional).

**Core Principle:**  
“Cinnamon is a **warming digestive and metabolic spice**, improving GI function and glycemic control while providing antimicrobial and circulatory support—but cassia forms carry a **coumarin‑linked liver risk at high chronic doses**.”[web:176][web:180][web:184]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Key Constituents:**

- **Cinnamaldehyde:** Main aromatic aldehyde; antimicrobial, vasodilatory, insulin signaling modulator.  
- **Eugenol (especially in leaf oil):** Analgesic, antimicrobial.  
- **Polyphenols:** Procyanidins, catechins with insulin‑sensitizing and antioxidant effects.  
- **Coumarin (cassia species):** Hepatotoxic at sufficiently high chronic doses; average ~0.5 mg/g bark with large variability.[web:176][web:184]

**Mechanisms:**

- **Insulin Sensitivity & Glycemia:**  
  - Cinnamomum cassia polyphenols improve insulin receptor signaling and glucose uptake; RCTs suggest modest reductions in fasting blood glucose and lipids with cinnamon supplementation.[web:176][web:180]  

- **Antimicrobial & Antifungal:**  
  - Cinnamaldehyde and cinnamon oil show significant activity against bacteria and fungi; widely used as preservative and antimicrobial flavoring.[web:176]

- **GI Carminative & Secretagogue:**  
  - Stimulates salivation, gastric and digestive secretions; relaxes smooth muscle spasm and reduces gas.

- **Coumarin Toxicology:**  
  - Coumarin is hepatotoxic in animals; EFSA TDI = 0.1 mg/kg/day for coumarin.[web:176][web:184]  
  - Analysis of cinnamon bark: ~0.5 mg coumarin per gram average; 4 g had ~12.1 mg coumarin in one study.[web:176]  
  - Some Kampo users exceeded TDI but showed *no abnormal liver function* in that cohort, suggesting risk is individual and context‑dependent.[web:176]

## KEY MECHANISMS

**1. Digestive Warming & Carminative**  
Cinnamon’s volatile oils stimulate digestion and peristalsis, helping resolve cold, sluggish digestion, gas, and diarrhea from cold damp.[web:180]  
Effect: Less bloating and discomfort, improved appetite, warmer core sensation.

**2. Blood Sugar Modulation (Cassia)**  
Cassia cinnamon 1–6 g/day has been used to modestly reduce fasting glucose and improve lipids in T2DM and metabolic syndrome in many trials, though results vary.[web:176][web:180]  
Effect: Improved glycemic control and possibly insulin sensitivity; magnitude modest but synergistic with diet/exercise.

**3. Antimicrobial & Food Safety**  
Cinnamon oil and cinnamaldehyde inhibit multiple pathogens, contributing both to GI health and food preservation.[web:176]

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION

**Food Level (Safe for Most):**

- 0.5–2 g/day of cinnamon powder in food, teas, or capsules is widely used.[web:180]  

**Medicinal Cassia Cinnamon (T2DM / Metabolic):**

- WebMD and other sources: **1–2 g/day** as medicine safely for up to 3 months; up to **6 g/day** used in some studies but regarded as potentially unsafe long‑term due to coumarin.[web:180]  

**True Cinnamon (C. verum):**

- Much lower coumarin; allows higher long‑term culinary use, but metabolic data less robust than cassia.

**Essential Oil (Internal):**

- Only under professional guidelines and typically in very small, diluted doses; not necessary for your tincture/beverage context.

**Duration:**

- For metabolic support: **8–12 weeks** then reassess, especially with cassia and liver status.[web:176][web:180]

**Example Protocols**

**Protocol 1: Warming Digestive Carminative (FOUNDATION)**  
- 0.5–1 g cinnamon powder per cup tea with ginger and cardamom, 1–2× daily after meals.  

**Protocol 2: Metabolic Syndrome / T2DM Adjunct (Cassia)**  
- 1–2 g cassia cinnamon powder daily (in divided doses) for 3 months, with liver function monitoring in at‑risk individuals.[web:176][web:180]

**Protocol 3: Cold‑Pattern Dysmenorrhea**  
- Small doses (0.5–1 g) of cinnamon powder in warming teas during cold, crampy menses, as part of a formula.

## KEY BENEFITS & CONTRAINDICATIONS

**Benefits:**  

- **Digestive warming and carminative** actions.  
- **Metabolic support** (modest glucose and lipid improvements, especially cassia).[web:176][web:180]  
- Antimicrobial and antioxidant properties.

**Safety Overview (Cassia Focus):**

- Food‑level use is **widely safe**.  
- Medicinal use up to 1–2 g/day for 3 months appears safe for most; doses >6 g/day long term may exceed coumarin TDI and be “possibly unsafe.”[web:180][web:176][web:184]

**⚠️ CRITICAL CAUTIONS**

- **Liver Disease / Coumarin Sensitivity:**  
  - People with existing liver disease are more susceptible to coumarin hepatotoxicity; limit cassia and prefer C. verum.[web:176][web:180][web:184]  

- **Pregnancy:**  
  - Culinary amounts fine; high doses as emmenagogue have traditional abortifacient concern—avoid high medicinal doses.

**MAJOR CAUTIONS**

- **Anticoagulant & Antiplatelet Therapy:**  
  - Cinnamon may mildly increase bleeding risk, especially when combined with high eugenol or other herbs/drugs affecting coagulation (the evidence is modest but caution warranted).

- **Hypoglycemic Therapy:**  
  - Cinnamon can improve glycemic control; monitor blood glucose and adjust meds as needed.[web:180]

**Drug Interactions:**  

- Antidiabetic meds (additive blood sugar lowering).[web:180]  
- Potential synergy with hepatotoxic drugs via cumulative liver burden (coumarin).
