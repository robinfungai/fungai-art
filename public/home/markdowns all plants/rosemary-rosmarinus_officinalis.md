## BOTANICAL & IDENTIFICATION

**Primary Species:**  
Salvia rosmarinus Spenn. (syn. Rosmarinus officinalis L.) — Woody evergreen shrub native to the Mediterranean; now cultivated widely in temperate regions. Leaves and flowering tops harvested during flowering for highest essential oil content.[web:35][web:29]

**Botanical Family:**  
Lamiaceae (mint family).[web:35]

**Part Used:**  
Rosmarini folium — Dried leaves (and young flowering tops) used as culinary herb and medicine; essential oil (aetheroleum) used externally and in aromatherapy.[web:35]

**Harvesting & Processing:**  
Aerial parts harvested on dry days, late morning; dried quickly in shade with good airflow to preserve volatile oils; stored whole and crumbled as needed.[web:35]

**Quality Indicators:**  
Needle-like leaves with strong, camphoraceous, pine-like aroma; high essential oil content (~1–2.5%); intense flavor. Color green-grey; minimal stem and woody material.[web:35][web:29]

**Storage:**  
Airtight in glass or tins, away from light and heat. Potency peak 1–2 years; essential oil content declines over time.[web:35]

## PHARMACOLOGICAL PROFILE

**Active Compounds:**  

**Primary Bioactives:**  
- **Essential oil components:** 1,8-cineole, camphor, α-pinene, borneol — stimulant, circulatory, mild bronchodilatory, antimicrobial.[web:35]  
- **Phenolic acids:** Rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid — strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.[web:35]  
- **Diterpenes:** Carnosic acid, carnosol — neuroprotective, antioxidant, possible anti-carcinogenic activities.[web:35]

**Mechanisms:**  
- **Cognitive & Circulatory Stimulation:** Essential oils enhance cerebral blood flow and cholinergic transmission, improving alertness, memory, and reaction time in some studies.[web:29]  
- **Antioxidant & Neuroprotective:** Rosmarinic acid and carnosic acid protect neurons from oxidative stress and excitotoxicity.[web:35]  
- **Digestive Stimulant & Carminative:** Bitter-aromatic profile increases gastric secretions and bile flow, aiding fat digestion and relieving gas.[web:29]  
- **Anti-Inflammatory & Antimicrobial:** Modulates inflammatory pathways (COX, LOX) and exhibits activity against bacteria and fungi.[web:35]

## DOSAGE & PREPARATION

**Tea/Infusion:**  
- 1–2 g dried leaves per 150–250 ml boiling water; infuse 10–15 minutes.[web:29][web:35]  
- Daily dose 2–6 g herb divided in 2–3 cups per day.[web:29][web:35]

**Tincture:**  
- 1:5 (40–60% alcohol): 1–4 ml 2–3× daily.

**Essential Oil (Internal Use):**  
- Highly cautious; internal EO not typically recommended in general practice. EMA monograph suggests theoretical 48–72 µl/day corresponding to 4–6 g herb, but many practitioners avoid internal EO due to toxicity risk.[web:35]  
- For app: restrict to external/aromatic use, not internal.

**Topical:**  
- Diluted EO in carrier oil (1–3%) for massage of sore muscles and scalp (hair growth support tradition).  

**Duration:**  
- Safe long-term in culinary doses; therapeutic doses typically used in 4–12 week blocks then reassessed.[web:35]

**Best Preparations & Protocols:**  

**Protocol 1: Cognitive & Focus Support**  
- 1 cup rosemary tea (1–2 g) in the morning; optional use of rosemary hydrosol or EO diffusion during mental work.  
- Combine with gotu kola, ginkgo, or bacopa for cognition.

**Protocol 2: Digestive Support Post-Fatty Meals**  
- 1 cup rosemary tea after meals or 1–2 ml tincture as aperitif/digestif; combine with bitter allies (artichoke, gentian) as needed.[web:29]

**Protocol 3: Musculoskeletal Ache & Cold Hands/Feet**

## CONTRAINDICATIONS & INTERACTIONS

**Safety Profile:**  
GRADE B+ — Very safe as a culinary herb; mild cautions at higher doses or in essential oil form.

**Contraindications / Cautions:**  
- **Pregnancy:** Avoid high internal doses and essential oil due to uterine-stimulating potential; culinary use fine.[web:35]  
- **Seizure Disorders:** High doses of camphor-rich EO may lower seizure threshold; avoid EO in epilepsy.[web:35]  
- **Hypertension:** Some caution with concentrated EO internal use (stimulant), but culinary and tea doses generally safe.

**Herb-to-Drug Interactions:**  
- Theoretical interactions with anticoagulants or antihypertensives at high doses due to circulatory effects; clinical significance unclear.[web:35]
