The Story

Chaga is the elder who reminds us that decay is a form of devotion. It grows only on birch that has been wounded — a spore drifts into a scar and, over ten to twenty years, quietly transmutes the tree's wound into a hard, coal-black conk shot through with amber and gold. The Sámi and northern Slavic peoples have brewed it as tea for centuries; the Russians formalised it in cold-war-era pharmacological research as Befungin.

The interesting move happens at the molecular level. Betulin and betulinic acid — concentrated in the outer bark of the birch — migrate into the chaga body and become bioavailable to us through the fungus (they aren't when we eat birch bark directly). The beta-glucan polysaccharides tune the innate immune system without over-stimulating it. The melanin content is among the highest in the natural world — a rare source of antioxidant pigmentation.

"Thriving in decay, weaving new foundations — shadow, resilience, slow transformation."

Chaga is not a rescue medicine. It's a long relationship. Take it as a daily practice through winter for the classical immune benefit, or as a year-round tonic for the cellular-defence and anti-inflammatory work. Effects are cumulative — measurable at 6–12 weeks, deep at a year.

What she carries
Betulinic acid · Betulin

Triterpenes concentrated from birch bark and made bioavailable through the fungus. Documented anti-inflammatory, anti-viral and anti-neoplastic activity in the research literature. The fat-soluble half of the medicine — only extracted by alcohol.

Beta-glucan polysaccharides

Tune the innate immune system — NK cell activity, macrophage function — without pushing it toward auto-immunity. The water-soluble half. This is why real chaga preparations must be double-extracted; alcohol-only extracts miss the polysaccharides entirely.

Melanin

Chaga contains among the highest melanin content of any known biological source. Powerful antioxidant, radiation-protective, protective of DNA. The reason chaga tea is so dark and inky.

SOD · Superoxide Dismutase

The body's primary internal antioxidant enzyme — chaga is one of the few natural sources with meaningful SOD activity. Supports the cellular defence layer that ordinary antioxidants can't reach.

How to take it

15–30 drops in warm water or under the tongue, once daily. Best in the morning — chaga has no stimulant note but a gentle warming quality that pairs with the start of the day.

For winter immunity: work with it consistently through the colder months. For long-term antioxidant defence: year-round is fine. Chaga is one of the very few tonic mushrooms with no known dose ceiling — safe for indefinite daily use.

Also used as the umami spine of the forest broth in the Dinner Experience.

Safety notes
  • Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs): chaga has mild antiplatelet activity. Monitor if you're on blood-thinning medication.
  • Autoimmune conditions: chaga is immune-modulating. Discuss with your clinician if you have RA, MS, lupus or similar.
  • Kidney disease with oxalate concerns: chaga contains oxalates. Not the primary tonic for anyone with a history of oxalate stones or advanced kidney disease.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: limited data. Traditional use supports safety but formal studies are absent. Discuss with your practitioner.
  • Compatible with: most pharmaceuticals, most other tonic herbs, most protocols. This is a foundationally safe herb with a long track record.
